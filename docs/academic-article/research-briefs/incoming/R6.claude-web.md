# R6 — Evaluation methodology, benchmarks, and ground truth for LLM malware analysis

> **Provenance.** Claude (Opus 5), live web search, 2026-08-08, against the current R6 brief.
> Body citations were resolved by fetching abstract pages; unverified leads are in §5.
> The **Chasing Shadows** lead came from `ALL8.model-a.md` — the one genuinely valuable thing
> that report produced for this theme, and it checks out.
>
> **Bottom line up front.** Our methodological results are real but they land in an area that
> already has a literature, and a strong one. *Chasing Shadows* (NDSS'26) surveyed **all 72**
> LLM-security papers from leading venues in 2023–2024 and found **every single one contains at
> least one of nine pitfalls**, with only 15.7% of those pitfalls acknowledged. Its author list
> includes Daniel Arp, of *Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security* — this is
> that critique's direct successor. Our N1 is a concrete instance inside a named tradition, not
> a new observation.
>
> That is good news for the paper's *framing* and bad news for its *novelty*: the honest move is
> to position our methodology results as **worked examples with numbers**, cite the tradition,
> and stop calling them discoveries.

## 1. State of the art

- **Chasing Shadows: Pitfalls in LLM Security Research** — Jonathan Evertz, Niklas Risse,
  Nicolai Neuer, Andreas Müller, Philipp Normann, Gaetano Sapia, Srishti Gupta, David Pape,
  Soumya Shaw, Devansh Srivastav, Christian Wressnegger, Erwin Quiring, Thorsten Eisenhofer,
  **Daniel Arp**, Lea Schönherr. **NDSS 2026**. `arXiv:2512.09549`
  - Prevalence study over **all 72 peer-reviewed papers** at leading Security and Software
    Engineering venues, **2023–2024**. Nine pitfalls *"span the entire computation process, from
    data collection, pre-training, and fine-tuning to prompting and evaluation."*
  - Headline, verbatim: *"Every paper contains at least one pitfall"*, and *"only 15.7% of the
    present pitfalls were explicitly discussed."*
  - Artifacts: GitHub (`Dormant-Neurons/llm-pitfalls`) and a living appendix at
    `llmpitfalls.org` — i.e. it is maintained, which makes it the reference to check ourselves
    against rather than a static citation.
  - Rigor flags: **full-population survey rather than a sample** | explicit prevalence
    accounting | artifacts released | CIs? n/a | stat test? n/a
  - Confidence: **HIGH** on existence, venue, scope and both quoted figures. **The nine pitfalls
    are not named in the abstract** — the full paper is required and it is the single most
    important document for this theme.

- **Dos and Don'ts of Machine Learning in Computer Security** — Arp et al. `arXiv:2010.09470`
  (USENIX Security 2022). Ten subtle pitfalls in ML-for-security, shown to affect validity and
  to cause **overestimated** performance. The ancestor of the above, and the reason the tradition
  exists.
  - Confidence: **HIGH** that it exists and is foundational; **MEDIUM** on details — surfaced by
    search, **not fetched**.

- **Measuring what Matters: Construct Validity in Large Language Model Benchmarks** —
  `arXiv:2511.04703`
  - Systematic review of **445 LLM benchmarks** from leading conferences, finding *"patterns
    related to the measured phenomena, tasks, and scoring metrics which undermine the validity
    of the resulting claims"*, and issuing eight recommendations.
  - A widely repeated figure from this work is that **only 16% used uncertainty estimates or
    statistical tests** — I saw this in a search summary, **not in the abstract I fetched**, so
    it is flagged `MEDIUM` and must be confirmed from the paper before we cite it. It is exactly
    the number our own rigor tallies have been groping toward, which is a reason to be *more*
    careful with it, not less.
  - Confidence: **HIGH** on existence and the 445 figure; **MEDIUM** on the 16%.

- **The Benchmarking Epistemology: Construct Validity for Evaluating Machine Learning Models** —
  `arXiv:2510.23191`. Argues benchmark scores measure performance relative to a dataset and a
  concrete learning problem, and that substantive scientific inference needs further assumptions
  about the theoretical structure of the problem. Confidence: **MEDIUM** — **not fetched**.

- **SoK: Automated TTP Extraction from CTI Reports — Are We There Yet?** — Büchel et al.,
  **USENIX Security 2025**, pp. 4621–4641. Verified in `R2.claude-web.md`. Belongs here as much
  as in R2: *"existing solutions are largely incomparable as they consider decisively different
  and constrained settings, rely on custom TTP ontologies, and use a multitude of custom,
  inaccessible CTI datasets"*, plus a **unified re-evaluation** and the conclusion that better
  datasets and ontological clarity are the way forward.

- **Benchmarks in and around this space** — TTPrint-Bench (150 full CTI reports, 12 sources,
  2022–2025, 125 techniques including 66 rare, `arXiv:2605.25836`, verified) and **TRAM-Clean**,
  a *cleaned* version of the standard benchmark from the same paper; **AnnoCTR** (LREC-COLING
  2024, `arXiv:2404.07765`, CC-BY-SA, verified); the 2,076-sentence human-annotated corpus with
  κ=0.68 in `arXiv:2606.18166` (verified). Agent-facing benchmarks surfaced but **not fetched**:
  SEC-bench, CVE-Bench, CTIBench, NYU CTF Bench, BinMetric, REBench, REFORGE.

### 1b. Rigor tally

This theme inverts the usual result: the papers *about* rigor are rigorous, and they are the ones
telling us the rest of the field is not.

| property | who |
|---|---|
| Full-population survey (not a sample) | Chasing Shadows — all 72 papers in scope |
| Prevalence accounting with an acknowledgement rate | Chasing Shadows — 15.7% discussed |
| Unified re-evaluation of prior systems | Büchel SoK — 40+ systems in one setting |
| Large-scale benchmark audit | `2511.04703` — 445 benchmarks |
| Released artifacts / living appendix | Chasing Shadows |
| Inter-annotator agreement | `2606.18166` (κ = 0.68) |
| Benchmark treated as a defect to be fixed | TTPrint (TRAM-Clean) |

**Most rigorous, named:** **Chasing Shadows** (population survey + artifacts + maintained
appendix), the **Büchel SoK** (the only unified cross-system re-evaluation in TTP extraction),
and `arXiv:2511.04703` (445-benchmark validity audit).

**Cross-theme observation worth carrying into the paper.** Counting across R2, R3, R4 and R5:
equal-budget controls appeared in **2 of ~18** individually verified papers, and both were the
multi-agent negative results in R3 — where the control *is* the finding. Statistical tests
appeared in 2 (`2606.18166`, and R3's design). Confidence intervals: **0**. That pattern is
consistent with what Chasing Shadows and `2511.04703` report at proper scale, which is
reassuring for our observation and fatal for its novelty.

### 1c. Model-tier tally

Not meaningful for this theme — these are surveys and audits, not systems. The relevant
observation is that **Chasing Shadows covers LLM-security papers generally**, so its pitfalls
apply to us whether or not we are in its 72.

## 2. Gaps in the literature

- **G1 — No per-sample ATT&CK ground truth for Windows/Linux binaries, in public.** `MEDIUM`
  - Evidence: every verified benchmark is CTI *prose* (TRAM/TRAM2, TTPrint-Bench, AnnoCTR,
    `2606.18166`). TTPDetect (`arXiv:2602.06325`) **built** a decompiled-function↔TTP dataset,
    which is exactly this — but its release status is unverified.
  - Severity: high, and it is the binding constraint on everyone's recall numbers including ours.
  - **Downgraded from `HIGH`:** the gap may already be closed by an unreleased artifact. Check
    before claiming.

- **G2 — Concept drift is not evaluated for LLM-based malware analysis.** `MEDIUM`
  - Evidence: the drift literature is about trained classifiers. I found no LLM-based drift
    study for malware analysis, and Chasing Shadows' pitfalls are about validity rather than
    temporal robustness.
  - Severity: moderate. This is the cleanest remaining slot for our E1.
  - Confidence limited by the usual problem: this is an absence.

- **G3 — Instrument-validity critiques exist at the field level, not the metric level.**
  `MEDIUM`
  - Evidence: Chasing Shadows and `2511.04703` critique *practice* and *construct validity*
    broadly. I found no worked example showing a specific metric inverting the ranking of the
    same systems under a nuisance parameter.
  - Severity: moderate — a demonstration is more persuasive than a category, and we have one.

## 3. Does the described approach close these gaps?

### Claim N1 — a single-run parsed-claim count is not a valid instrument

- **Prior work claiming a similar solution:** **Chasing Shadows** (`arXiv:2512.09549`, NDSS'26)
  and **Arp et al.** (`arXiv:2010.09470`) establish the pitfall tradition for LLM-security and
  ML-security respectively; **`arXiv:2511.04703`** and **`arXiv:2510.23191`** establish construct
  validity as the frame.
  *How I searched:* security-first, then the general **measurement-validity** vocabulary
  ("construct validity", "benchmark critique") — which is where the ML-wide audits live.
- **Verdict: `PARTIALLY`.** The *category* is thoroughly occupied. What I found no instance of is
  the specific demonstration: **the same arms ranked in contradictory orders across three runs
  because the decoding budget changed**, with the metric held fixed. That is a construct-validity
  failure caught in the act, with the nuisance parameter identified.
- The honest framing: *"an instance of the pitfall class named by Evertz et al., measured"* —
  which is publishable as a worked example and citable, but is not a discovery.

### Claim E1 — 7-year drift study, n=210, no measurable drift

- **Prior work claiming a similar result: `NONE FOUND`** for LLM-based malware analysis.
  Drift evaluation is standard for trained classifiers; I found no LLM equivalent.
- **Verdict: `NOT CLOSED` by prior work — our strongest surviving empirical claim.**
- Three caveats that must ship with it, all of which Chasing Shadows would flag if we did not:
  - **the ground truth is family-level**, so absolute recall carries a structural ceiling and
    only the *relative delta* is claimable — we already state this, and now it can be cited to
    the SoK's own dataset critique rather than asserted;
  - **a null result needs power**, and "all CIs overlap" is not the same as "no drift exists";
    an equivalence-style statement would be stronger than an absence of significance;
  - **one model, one machine** — the drift we measured is the drift of *this* deployment.

### Our own rigor, measured against the tradition

Applying Chasing Shadows' own standard to ourselves, honestly:

| what the tradition asks | where we stand |
|---|---|
| Report CIs and repeats | **done** in §3.5, §3.6, §4 Item 5 — better than most of what R2–R5 surfaced |
| Equal-budget controls | **done** in §3.6; **absent** where it matters most (E.2, unmeasured) |
| Baselines | **weak** — no baseline system anywhere (E.4) |
| Instrument validity | **done**, and it is N1 |
| Acknowledge pitfalls explicitly | Chasing Shadows found only 15.7% of pitfalls acknowledged; our findings log's status tags and retractions are unusually strong here, and that is worth showing rather than saying |
| Data leakage / contamination | **partially** — the family-fingerprint catalog built from the eval corpus was caught and documented, but not systematically audited |

The last row is the one to act on: a short, explicit **"pitfalls we checked ourselves against"**
section, keyed to the nine, would convert our documentation discipline into a defensible claim —
and it is nearly free, because the material is already in the findings log.

## 4. How to extend to close the remaining gaps

1. **Read *Chasing Shadows* in full and self-audit against its nine pitfalls.** *Effort: low,
   `[cheap]`.* It has released artifacts and a living appendix, so this is a checklist exercise.
   Highest value-per-hour item in the entire review: it tells us what a 2026 security reviewer
   is now primed to look for, and our findings log already contains most of the answers.
   Reviewer's counter: none — this only strengthens the paper.

2. **Restate N1 as a worked example, not a discovery.** *Effort: trivial.* "An instance of
   pitfall X measured in the wild" is a strong, citable contribution; "we found that metrics can
   be invalid" is not.

3. **Strengthen E1 from a null result into an equivalence claim.** *Effort: low, statistical
   only — the data exists.* Overlapping CIs are not evidence of absence. A TOST-style bound
   ("drift is smaller than δ F1 over 7 years") is a positive claim from the same numbers.
   Reviewer's counter: *"underpowered"* — which the bound answers directly.

4. **Check whether TTPDetect's dataset is released.** *Effort: trivial.* Appears in R2 and R5 as
   well; it decides G1 and the fate of a survey claim in three places.

5. **Do not attempt to build a benchmark.** *Effort: very high, value: uncertain.* The SoK says
   dataset quality is the field's blocker, so a good one would be a major contribution — and
   also a different paper, requiring annotation infrastructure and inter-annotator agreement we
   do not have. Recording it as an explicit non-goal is worth more than a half-built corpus.
