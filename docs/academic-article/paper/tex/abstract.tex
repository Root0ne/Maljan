A large language model (LLM) pipeline for mapping malware evidence to MITRE ATT\&CK techniques is mostly not the model: it wraps one in a
sandbox, a disassembler, retrieval indices and rule engines. What the model contributes is a
difference against that tooling rather than a score, and reading one needs four cheap checks: a baseline to refer an F1 to, a firing rate before any
ablation, a minimum detectable effect beside every null, and a count of distinct outputs. We apply
them component by component. Three findings are positive. Splitting the analysis across several model calls scores
\fact{cape-negotiated-delta} F1 above a single judge on \fact{cape-consensus-families} real samples, one per family, at
\fact{cape-consensus-negotiated-ratio}$\times$ the output. A technique assigner composing its ranking and gating backends, rather than choosing between them,
gives the cleanest gate on two external corpora. And turning one decoding flag off gains \fact{reasoning-flag-worth-low} to
\fact{reasoning-flag-worth-high} F1, more than any architecture or size measured. Four
are negative or bounded. The negotiation it was built for contributes \fact{cape-mechanism-delta} at a resolution of
\fact{mde-cape-mechanism}: what pays is the calls, not the argument. Against that
sandbox the full pipeline is \fact{h2h-delta} F1, at a resolution of \fact{h2h-mde}. Three retrieval components move nothing end to end. And the confidence every gate consumes separates correct from incorrect claims at an area under the
curve of \fact{confidence-auc}, on an interval containing chance. The fourth check found four of
seven defects in our own instrument, each returning a plausible result rather than an error.
